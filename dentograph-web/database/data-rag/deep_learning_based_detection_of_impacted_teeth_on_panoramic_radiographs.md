## Abstract

### Objective

The study aimed to detect impacted teeth in panoramic radiographs by refining a pretrained MedSAM model.

### Study Design

Impacted teeth can cause complications and are commonly diagnosed using radiographs. The authors modified the Segment Anything Model for individual tooth segmentation using 1016 panoramic X-ray images. The dataset was divided into training, validation, and testing sets using a ratio of 16:3:1. The model was enhanced to detect impacted teeth automatically by focusing on the center of each tooth.

### Results

Training used 200 epochs, batch size 1, and learning rate 0.001. On the test set, the best SAM-family model achieved an accuracy of 86.73%, an F1 score of 0.5350, and an intersection-over-union value of 0.3652.

### Conclusion

Fine-tuning MedSAM improved impacted-tooth segmentation in panoramic radiographs and may support dental diagnosis. Further improvements in model accuracy and model selection are needed.

**Keywords:** computer-aided detection; impacted teeth; zero-shot; MedSAM

## Introduction

Impacted teeth are among the most common dental problems and affect people of different ages. They are frequently evaluated and managed by oral and maxillofacial surgeons. Reported prevalence ranges from 0.8% to 3.6%, with third molars being the teeth most commonly involved.

Periapical and panoramic radiographs are generally used to diagnose impacted teeth. Cone-beam computed tomography provides three-dimensional imaging, but it is less convenient and more expensive for routine impacted-tooth diagnosis. Panoramic radiography remains widely used because of its accessibility and suitability for initial evaluation.

Advances in computer technology have transformed radiology by improving the standardization of diagnosis and enabling new imaging methods. The growing number of panoramic dental images and the limited availability of clinicians create demand for computer-assisted diagnostic systems. Artificial intelligence and neural networks have therefore become important components of computer-aided health care and medical-image object detection.

Previous dental AI studies have mainly addressed caries, periodontitis, implants, restorative dentistry, and orthodontics. Fewer studies have focused specifically on impacted teeth. Earlier systems have used deep convolutional neural networks, AlexNet, VGG-16, DetectNet, R-CNN Inception v2, Faster R-CNN, and YOLOv3. Reported results include 86.2% accuracy for locating impacted third molars, 96% accuracy for detecting impacted supernumerary teeth, an F1 score of 86.25% for impacted-tooth charting, and 96% mean average precision for impacted mandibular third-molar detection.

The present study proposed detecting impacted teeth in panoramic images by pretraining and fine-tuning the Segment Anything Model on dental X-ray images. The original SAM can perform automated segmentation using box or point prompts, but its zero-shot capability is not sufficient to reliably distinguish impacted teeth from healthy teeth.

The proposed method first predicts the approximate mask of an impacted tooth. The center of mass of each predicted mask is then extracted and used as a landmark or point prompt for a second SAM-family model. The purpose is to obtain complete segmentation of the individual tooth and identify impacted teeth whose positions may be inconspicuous.

The study applies zero-shot segmentation to improve diagnostic efficiency and reduce the amount of task-specific annotation required. The authors also refined the model through specialized pretraining and fine-tuning on dental X-ray images to extend the applicability of SAM in dentistry.

## Materials and Methods

### Dataset and Preprocessing

The dataset was provided by DENTEX, a MICCAI 2023 competition dataset. It contains three annotation levels:

1. Quadrant information.
2. Quadrant and tooth-enumeration information.
3. Quadrant, enumeration, and disease labels.

The disease annotations contain four categories. Images labeled as impacted teeth were selected for this study.

Segmentation masks were generated from the annotation JSON files by connecting the coordinates of mask points. The enclosed area was assigned the foreground value and the rest of the image was assigned the background value.

To increase the size and variability of the dataset, 254 images were augmented using:

- Random noise.
- Rotation.
- Horizontal or vertical flipping.
- Color inversion.
- Brightness modification.

After augmentation, the dataset contained 1016 panoramic X-ray samples.

### Model Architecture

The initial experiments used the original Segment Anything Model to segment impacted teeth. Its zero-shot predictions often differed from expert ground-truth annotations, particularly when distinguishing impacted teeth from healthy teeth. One reason was that the original SAM training data did not contain impacted teeth. MedSAM was adapted for medical imaging, but its training data also did not specifically cover oral radiographs.

The authors therefore fine-tuned SAM following the MedSAM methodology. MedSAM uses interactive prompt-based learning in which prompts are derived from masks. It also uses masked autoencoding as an objective for task-neutral self-supervised learning.

The fine-tuned model was trained on 1016 labeled panoramic X-ray images to predict possible impacted-tooth regions. This first model produced masks around the relative positions of impacted teeth but did not always segment the full tooth.

To obtain complete tooth segmentation, the center of gravity of every predicted mask was calculated. These centroid locations were then used as point prompts for the following SAM-family models:

- SAM-Med2D.
- SAM-base.
- SAM-huge.
- SAM-large.
- MedSAM.

### Proposed Workflow

The proposed system consists of two SAM stages and one Mass Prediction Component:

1. A panoramic radiograph is provided to the fine-tuned SAM.
2. The fine-tuned SAM produces a position mask for each suspected impacted tooth.
3. The Mass Prediction Component calculates the centroid of each mask.
4. The centroids are used as point prompts for an original SAM-family model.
5. The second SAM model produces complete segmentation of each impacted tooth.
6. The final radiograph is returned with impacted teeth annotated.

This workflow allows one-click prediction and localization of potential impacted teeth.

> **Figure 2.** Workflow of the proposed structure and the SAM-family model. The fine-tuned SAM generates a position mask, the Mass Prediction Component creates centroid prompts, and a second SAM-family model performs final tooth segmentation.

### Training Configuration

The fine-tuned SAM was trained using:

- **Epochs:** 200.
- **Batch size:** 1.
- **Learning rate:** 0.001.
- **Total images:** 1016.
- **Validation set:** 15%.
- **Test set:** 5%.
- **Remaining images:** Training set.

### Evaluation Metrics

The model was evaluated using precision, accuracy, recall, F1 score, and intersection over union.

```text
Precision = TP / (TP + FP)

Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + FP + TN + FN)

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

F1 score = 2 × Precision × Recall / (Precision + Recall)

IoU = TP / (TP + FP + FN)
```

Where:

- **TP:** True positive.
- **TN:** True negative.
- **FP:** False positive.
- **FN:** False negative.

## Results

After the fine-tuned SAM detected an impacted-tooth region, the center of gravity of the predicted mask was used as the landmark for the second segmentation stage.

MedSAM obtained the highest values across all reported evaluation metrics. It achieved:

- **Accuracy:** 0.8673 or 86.73%.
- **F1 score:** 0.5350.
- **IoU:** 0.3652.
- **Recall:** 0.3868.

SAM-large produced the second-best results, while SAM-Med2D, SAM-base, and SAM-huge showed lower performance. The authors attributed this partly to the limited generality of zero-shot segmentation for this specialized task.

The model occasionally recognized several impacted teeth that may have matched potential lesions. However, the training and test masks annotated only the most prominent impacted tooth in each image. Consequently, the model’s ability to segment multiple impacted teeth could not be fully evaluated.

## Discussion

The study evaluated fine-tuned MedSAM for impacted-tooth segmentation using a centroid-extraction approach. Fine-tuning with panoramic X-ray images and mask-derived markers improved detection performance, with the best model reaching 86.73% accuracy.

The findings demonstrate that a pretrained general-purpose segmentation model can be adapted to a specialized dental task using a relatively small dataset. This is important because dental datasets are often limited in size and contain highly specific anatomical features.

The use of the predicted-mask centroid as a prompt allowed the second SAM stage to segment the full impacted tooth more accurately. This approach combined task-specific localization from the fine-tuned model with the segmentation capability of the original SAM-family models.

The results also support the feasibility of zero-shot or prompt-based segmentation in dental imaging. However, the lower F1 score, recall, and IoU compared with accuracy indicate that accurate pixel-level segmentation remains challenging.

### Limitations

The study identified several limitations:

1. The dataset was relatively small, which may reduce generalizability.
2. The dataset might not represent all types and positions of impacted teeth.
3. The demographic distribution may not represent wider clinical populations.
4. Only the most prominent impacted tooth was annotated in each image.
5. Multiple-tooth segmentation was therefore not fully evaluated.
6. Additional parameter tuning and comparison with other segmentation architectures are required.

Future studies should use larger and more diverse datasets, improve multi-tooth annotations, refine model parameters, compare additional SAM-family models, and evaluate integration with other dental diagnostic systems.

---

## Conclusion

Fine-tuning the Segment Anything Model improved segmentation of impacted teeth on panoramic radiographs. The proposed two-stage workflow combines impacted-tooth localization, centroid extraction, and prompt-based full-tooth segmentation.

The model may support clinical decision-making and treatment planning by highlighting subtle impacted teeth. The study also demonstrates the potential of integrating advanced deep-learning segmentation methods into dental practice, although further validation and performance improvements are required before routine clinical implementation.
